♻️ Kitchen fertilisers

Reuse everyday kitchen scraps to make compost and improve your soil. These are soil amendments, not complete fertilisers.

🍌 Banana peels

Best use: Add chopped peels to a working compost bin and let them break down fully before using the compost.

How: Chop into small pieces and mix with dry leaves or shredded cardboard. Do not rely on buried fresh peels as a quick potassium feed.

Suitable for compost destined for fruit trees, vegetables and ornamentals.

🥚 Eggshells

Best use: Compost them, or crush very finely and add modestly to the compost.

How: Rinse, dry and crush. They break down slowly, so they will not quickly correct a calcium deficiency or change soil pH.

Do not use eggshells as a substitute for diagnosing blossom-end rot in tomatoes; inconsistent watering is a common factor.

☕ Used coffee grounds

Best use: Add small amounts to compost and mix well with dry brown material.

How: Let grounds cool, then sprinkle thinly into the compost. Avoid thick layers or piling wet grounds against plant stems.

Used grounds are not a reliable acidifier for citrus, blueberries or other acid-loving plants.

🍵 Tea leaves

Best use: Add loose tea leaves to compost.

How: Empty the leaves from the bag if it contains plastic or synthetic mesh. Mix with dry leaves or cardboard.

🥬 Fruit and vegetable scraps

Best use: Make mature compost for the garden.

How: Chop scraps small and layer with dry leaves, shredded untreated cardboard or paper. Keep the mix moist like a wrung-out sponge and turn it to add air.

Use finished, earthy-smelling compost as a thin top-dressing around plants, keeping it away from trunks and stems.

🍚 Rice-rinse water

Recommendation: Do not treat this as a dependable fertiliser. Its nutrient content is low and variable. If you reuse plain, unsalted water, use it sparingly on outdoor soil only, and stop if it smells or attracts pests.

🌿 Simple kitchen-scrap compost recipe

  1. Collect fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds and tea leaves.
  2. Mix with plenty of dry leaves, torn cardboard or other suitable brown material.
  3. Keep it damp, not soggy, and turn or mix regularly for airflow.
  4. Wait until it is dark, crumbly and smells earthy before using it.
  5. Spread a thin layer on the soil surface; do not pile against trunks.
Important safety: For a standard home compost bin, keep out meat, fish, dairy, oils, salty foods, pet waste and diseased plants. These can cause odours, attract pests or create hygiene problems. Follow your local council's composting rules. Kitchen-scrap compost improves soil but does not supply every nutrient in the right balance; use a suitable fertiliser when a plant needs one.

New Zealand guidance: Wellington City Council composting · Ministry for the Environment food scraps guide.
